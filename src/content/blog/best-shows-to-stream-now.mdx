There is more prestige television in a single month now than most people watched in a year a decade ago. That is the problem. The catalogues are enormous, the algorithms push whatever is newest, and "trending" rarely means "good." This is a shortlist you can actually finish — a handful of shows that earn the hours, organised by the mood you're in when you sit down.

## When you want to be gripped

**A tense, single-location thriller** is the safest bet for a night when your attention is thin. Look for limited series in the six-to-eight-episode range: they are written to a fixed ending, so the pacing rarely sags the way an open-ended drama does around season three. The tell of a good one is that the first episode gives you a concrete question — who did it, will they get out, what is in the room — and the finale actually answers it.

If you have the patience for something slower, the current crop of **procedural dramas with a serialised spine** has quietly become excellent. The case-of-the-week gives you an easy on-ramp, and the season-long arc rewards you for staying. This is the format to put on when you want something you can half-watch on a tired Tuesday and still follow.

## When you want to laugh without thinking

The **half-hour comedy** is having a genuine renaissance, and it is the most under-rated thing on streaming right now. Twenty-two minutes is the perfect unit: long enough to build a world, short enough that a weak episode costs you nothing. The best current comedies have figured out that warmth beats cynicism — the shows people rewatch are the ones where you'd want to spend time with the characters, not the ones that are merely clever.

A practical tip: comedies are where the streaming services hide their best writing on the smallest budgets, so this is the category where the lesser-known platforms punch above their weight. Do not skip a show because it is on a service you forgot you subscribe to.

## When you want to be transported

For **big-swing genre television** — science fiction, fantasy, prestige horror — the calculus is different. These shows live or die on their production design and their willingness to commit to a premise. The failure mode is a gorgeous pilot followed by a season that runs out of ideas. Before you invest, check whether the show is adapted from finished source material. Adaptations of completed books tend to have a shape; original genre shows are gambling that the writers can invent an ending before the budget runs out.

## How to actually choose

A few rules that have saved me more evenings than any recommendation engine:

- **Read the episode count before the reviews.** A tight limited series is almost always a better use of a night than the opening of a sprawling saga you may never finish.
- **Watch two episodes, not one.** Pilots are written under different pressure than the rest of a season. The second episode tells you what the show is actually going to be, week to week.
- **Ignore the top row.** The auto-playing hero banner is an advertising slot, not a recommendation. The good stuff is usually two or three rows down, or on the service you were about to cancel.
- **Let one show finish before you start the next.** The reason nothing feels satisfying is often that you're three episodes into five different series. Monogamy, briefly, is underrated.

## The short version

If you want gripped, pick a finished limited thriller. If you want to laugh, trust a half-hour comedy on a smaller service. If you want to be transported, favour genre shows adapted from completed books. And whatever you choose, watch two episodes before you decide it isn't for you — the medium's best work almost never lands in the first thirty minutes.

The catalogue will still be enormous tomorrow. The trick is not to watch everything. It's to finish something good.
